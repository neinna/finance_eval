Financial OS: where am I?

AI adoption in personal finance will start small: plugging into the tools people already use, reading their current state, and letting them talk to it. The long-term goal, I believe, is an operating system for your life, built and run by AI.

In finance the question is almost always a version of the same thing: here's what I have, here are my goals, how am I doing? The resources are similar from person to person and so are the goals; the details are not. The inputs look alike, the path is personal. That is exactly what AI is good at: take all of a person's data in and build an answer for that one person.

I see this every day in the personal-finance Facebook groups I'm in. People post their numbers and ask for a recommendation for their exact situation. The end state is a financial OS that holds all of my variables in one system of record, acts where it's allowed to, and tells me what I'm missing when I need it. That includes answering the questions I didn't know I needed to ask.

Inna Raykhman · October 2026 · built with Claude · tested on Gemini 3.1 Pro (preview) with Google Search

How this was built

Project overview

Claude built this project and wrote and updated the prompts, based on my thesis, my definition of what good looks like, and the sample questions. An AI PM has to understand the principles of writing good prompts, but AI can now be guided to write them, and that's what was done here.

The bet

The question I didn't know to ask

Gemini can now connect to Google Wallet and Plaid-linked accounts and read the current state of a person's finances. That's the first step toward a financial OS. The next step is making this data actionable, like surfacing what the user didn't know to look for: a Medicare surcharge two years out, a tax-free window closing in three, receipts that can be reimbursed today.

This is a small first test of how Gemini does this today, starting with answers that can be checked with numbers.

What was tested

16 cases

  • 16 cases, 9 with made-up documents, based on questions in public personal-finance groups: retirement, tax, housing, kids' accounts, insurance, savings, tools, finding advice. All personal details and numbers changed.
  • What good looks like: the answer surfaces what the user didn't know to ask (50 hidden facts across the cases) and gets the numbers right (20 numbers, checked against an answer key in code).
  • Two runs. Run 1 used the question as written. Run 2 added a rule set for Gemini to follow: read the documents, lead with what they may not know, ask at most three questions, default the rest, show the math, separate known from assumed.
  • Graded by Claude, not Gemini. In my coding harness, the same model reviews in a separate context because tests are the referee. Here the grade is a judgment, so I used a different model that doesn't share Gemini's blind spots. Every grade quotes the sentence it's based on, and every number is checked against code, not a model.

Results

The harness matters more than the model

The rule set in run 2 made the difference, not the model size. In run 1, a smaller model (Gemini 3.6 Flash, no search) caught about as many hidden facts as Pro on the 9 cases both ran: 78% vs 80%.

What came back

Four things I learned

1. With the updated prompt, it found the unasked question that changes the decision

A 45-year-old is planning to stop working at 50. In run 1, Gemini says "You have likely already won the game," meaning he's all set, with a retirement budget that has no health insurance for a family of four. In run 2, it adds $24,000 a year for it, so the retirement math holds.

2. In run 1, Gemini never interviews the user

In 16 answers in run 1, Gemini asked the person nothing. Instead it guessed, sometimes a lot: it assumed the 45-year-old keeps saving $300K to $400K a year from a business he says is about to shrink. In run 2, every answer asked two or three questions and said what it assumed.

3. Number retrieval is inconsistent

Even with search on, Gemini used 2025 tax brackets labeled as 2026 in three answers. In one, it changed the advice: it said you can convert $33,450 and stay in the 22% bracket. The right number for 2026 is $35,800. The same model got it right in the other run. Tax figures change every year. They should live in a rules layer the model reads from, not in the model.

4. More useful means more directive

In run 2, answers commit: "cap it at $84,000 this year," "Do not shop around," "Sell the condo." That's what people in the groups are asking for. It's also exactly where information turns into advice, where a product has to decide what it will and won't say, and what the compliant version of the answer is.

What still broke

  • Adding up spending, the thing Gemini does today. Asked for health-care spending across three exports, both runs said $2,606.47. They counted drugstore purchases and Venmo payments as medical without checking. The sure-medical total is $1,318.00.
  • Prices that change between answers. The same budgeting app came back as "~$120/yr" in one answer and "about $40 to $50 a year" in another, neither with a source.
  • Financial aid. Neither run mentioned that a teen's UTMA account counts heavily against college aid.

What's next

Building a financial OS

The model reasons well when it has the facts and the right instructions. What's missing is the system around it:

  1. A thorough first interview. What are the goals, who's in the household, what changes down the road could matter.
  2. A household profile that stays. People, ages, accounts, income, goals with dates. Not re-asked every time.
  3. Deterministic rules that run on it. This year's tax figures, deadlines, limits. That's where the hidden facts come from, and it fixes the old-numbers problem.
  4. On track or not? Where am I against each goal.
  5. Proactive updates about my situation. Personal news and warnings tied to what I own and what I'm trying to do.

How much the financial OS does on its own is a dial, set by a number of factors, including regulation.
